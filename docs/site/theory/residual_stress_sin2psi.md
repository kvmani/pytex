# Residual Stress by the sin²ψ Method

This note derives the residual-stress evaluation implemented in
`pytex.diffraction.xrd_residual_stress` and exposed in the workbench as
**XRD → Residual stress (sin²ψ)**: how the position of one reflection, measured at several
specimen tilts $\psi$ and azimuths $\varphi$, determines the macroscopic stress tensor of the
irradiated layer, which elastic constants connect the two, how each peak is located, and how
every source of uncertainty reaches the answer.

Symbols follow {doc}`../standards/terminology_and_symbol_registry` (section *Residual stress by
the sin²ψ method*). Standard uncertainty is written $u(x)$ throughout, following the GUM, because
$\sigma$ is the stress. The algorithm and the report are described in
{doc}`../algorithms/residual_stress_sin2psi`; the precise spacing machinery it builds on is in
{doc}`precise_lattice_parameter_determination`.

## 1. Why a single scan cannot measure a stress

A symmetric $\theta$–$2\theta$ scan measures the spacing of the planes whose normal is the surface
normal $S_3$. At a free surface the stress is (to a very good approximation over the few
micrometres X-rays penetrate) biaxial, $\sigma_{i3} = 0$, and it reaches those planes only
through the Poisson contraction,

$$
\varepsilon_{33} = S_{1}\,(\sigma_{11} + \sigma_{22}),
$$

a strain of order $10^{-4}$ that is indistinguishable from a change of $d_{0}$ by composition,
temperature or instrument calibration. The stress becomes measurable only when planes *inclined*
to the surface are brought into the diffracting condition, by tilting the specimen. The spacing
is the strain gauge; the tilt orients it.

## 2. Geometry and the measured strain

```{figure} ../../figures/sin2psi_geometry.svg
:alt: The specimen frame S1 S2 S3, the scattering vector m at azimuth phi and tilt psi, and the diffracting planes normal to it.
:width: 88%

The measurement geometry, generated by `scripts/generate_residual_stress_figures.py` from
`measurement_direction`, the function the analysis uses. $\varphi$ is measured in the surface from
$S_1$ towards $S_2$; $\psi$ from the surface normal $S_3$.
```

The specimen frame is right-handed with $S_1, S_2$ in the surface and $S_3$ its outward normal. The
diffracting planes are those whose normal lies along the scattering vector

$$
\mathbf{m}(\varphi,\psi) = (\cos\varphi\sin\psi,\; \sin\varphi\sin\psi,\; \cos\psi).
$$

Bragg's law gives the spacing, $d_{\varphi\psi} = \lambda / (2\sin\theta_{\varphi\psi})$, and the
lattice strain along $\mathbf{m}$ is

$$
\varepsilon_{\varphi\psi} = \frac{d_{\varphi\psi} - d_{0}}{d_{0}}
= \mathbf{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{m}
= \varepsilon_{11}\cos^{2}\varphi\sin^{2}\psi + \varepsilon_{12}\sin 2\varphi\sin^{2}\psi
+ \varepsilon_{22}\sin^{2}\varphi\sin^{2}\psi + \varepsilon_{33}\cos^{2}\psi
+ \varepsilon_{13}\cos\varphi\sin 2\psi + \varepsilon_{23}\sin\varphi\sin 2\psi .
$$

The engineering definition $(d - d_{0})/d_{0}$ is used rather than $\ln(d/d_{0})$; at strains of
$10^{-3}$ the two differ by $5\times10^{-7}$, far below any measurement discussed here.

A negative tilt at azimuth $\varphi$ is the direction at azimuth $\varphi + 180^{\circ}$ and tilt
$|\psi|$. Nothing in the equations distinguishes the two descriptions; the sign convention matters
only because the $\sin 2\psi$ terms change sign with $\psi$.

## 3. From strain to stress: the diffraction elastic constants

For an elastically isotropic solid, Hooke's law
$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\boldsymbol{\sigma} - \frac{\nu}{E}(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\sigma})\mathbf{I}$
projected on $\mathbf{m}$ gives

$$
\varepsilon_{\varphi\psi} = \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{m}
+ S_{1}\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\sigma},
\qquad \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2} = \frac{1+\nu}{E},\quad S_{1} = -\frac{\nu}{E}.
$$

Real crystals are anisotropic, and the grains that diffract at $(\varphi,\psi)$ are a special
subset: those with the plane normal of $\{hkl\}$ along $\mathbf{m}$, at every rotation about it.
For an untextured (quasi-isotropic) aggregate the same two-constant form still holds, with
**reflection-dependent** diffraction (X-ray) elastic constants $S_{1}(hkl)$ and
$\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}(hkl)$ (Welzel et al. 2005). They are the subject of section 7; for ferrite
$\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}$ of $\{200\}$ exceeds that of $\{222\}$ by some 40 % under the Reuss model, and
a stress computed with the constants of the wrong reflection is wrong by the same factor.

Expanding $\mathbf{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{m}$ gives the **fundamental equation of
X-ray stress analysis**:

$$
\varepsilon_{\varphi\psi} = \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\left[(\sigma_{\varphi} - \sigma_{33})\sin^{2}\psi
+ \tau_{\varphi}\sin 2\psi\right] + \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\,\sigma_{33}
+ S_{1}(\sigma_{11} + \sigma_{22} + \sigma_{33}),
$$

$$
\sigma_{\varphi} = \sigma_{11}\cos^{2}\varphi + \sigma_{12}\sin 2\varphi + \sigma_{22}\sin^{2}\varphi,
\qquad \tau_{\varphi} = \sigma_{13}\cos\varphi + \sigma_{23}\sin\varphi .
$$

Every measurement is therefore **linear in the six stress components**. Writing the free
components as a vector $\mathbf{x}$, the measurements are $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$
with one row of $\mathbf{A}$ per $(\varphi,\psi)$ (`strain_design_matrix`). The stress follows by
linear least squares: no starting guess, no local minima, and an analytic covariance.

## 4. The sin²ψ law

Under plane stress, $\sigma_{i3} = 0$,

$$
d_{\varphi\psi} = d_{0}\left[1 + \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\,\sigma_{\varphi}\sin^{2}\psi
+ S_{1}(\sigma_{11} + \sigma_{22})\right].
$$

**$d$ is linear in $\sin^{2}\psi$ and the slope is the stress:**

$$
\frac{\partial d_{\varphi\psi}}{\partial \sin^{2}\psi} = d_{0}\,\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\,\sigma_{\varphi}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sigma_{\varphi} = \frac{1}{d_{0}\,\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}}\,\frac{\partial d}{\partial \sin^{2}\psi}.
$$

Three consequences carry the whole method:

1. **The slope is insensitive to $d_{0}$.** $d_{0}$ enters only as a scale, so a relative error
   $\delta = \Delta d_{0}/d_{0}$ changes $\sigma_{\varphi}$ by the fraction $\delta$. Replacing
   $d_{0}$ by the measured $d$ at $\psi = 0$ changes it by $S_{1}(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22})$, a few
   parts in $10^{4}$. This is why the method works on real alloys whose $d_{0}$ is not known to
   better than a part in $10^{4}$.
2. **The intercept is not.** $d(\sin^{2}\psi = 0) = d_{0}[1 + S_{1}(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22})]$
   carries the in-plane sum through $S_{1}$, and a $d_{0}$ error of $\delta$ reads as
   $\Delta(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22}) = \delta / S_{1}$. With $S_{1}\approx -1.25\ \mathrm{TPa}^{-1}$,
   $\delta = 10^{-4}$ is 80 MPa. In the joint tensor fit (section 9) the intercepts are part of the
   information, which is why $d_{0}$ appears in the uncertainty budget of $\sigma_{11}$ and
   $\sigma_{22}$ and hardly at all in that of the slopes.
3. **The strain-free direction.** The line crosses $d = d_{0}$ at
   $\sin^{2}\psi^{*} = -S_{1}(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22}) / (\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\sigma_{\varphi})$, which
   for an equibiaxial stress is $-2S_{1}/\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}$, independent of the stress. Measuring
   $d$ there is the classical way of obtaining a $d_{0}$ from the stressed specimen itself; the
   refinement of section 10 generalizes it.

## 5. ψ-splitting and out-of-plane shear

A shear stress $\tau_{\varphi}$ adds $\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\,\tau_{\varphi}\sin 2\psi$, which is **odd in
$\psi$**: plotted against $\sin^{2}\psi$ the measurements at $+\psi$ and $-\psi$ fall on two
different curves. With both signs measured at each $|\psi|$, the Dölle–Hauk evaluation separates
them exactly:

$$
a_{1} = \frac{d_{+} + d_{-}}{2} = d_{0}\left[1 + \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}(\sigma_{\varphi}-\sigma_{33})\sin^{2}\psi
+ \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\sigma_{33} + S_{1}\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right],
\qquad
a_{2} = \frac{d_{+} - d_{-}}{2} = d_{0}\,\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\,\tau_{\varphi}\sin|2\psi| .
$$

$a_{1}$ is linear in $\sin^{2}\psi$ and free of shear; $a_{2}$ is a straight line through the origin
in $\sin|2\psi|$ whose slope is $d_{0}\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\tau_{\varphi}$. The per-azimuth regression
(`fit_sin2psi_lines`) fits $d = c_{0} + c_{1}\sin^{2}\psi + c_{2}\sin 2\psi$ directly, which is the
same estimator without requiring every $|\psi|$ to be measured at both signs.

With $d_{0}$ known, $\sin^{2}\psi$ and $\sin 2\psi$ are independent functions even over
$\psi > 0$ alone, so the shear components are identifiable from one-sided tilts. They then rest on
the *curvature* of one branch rather than on the difference of two, and their variance is several
times larger; the library reports that honestly rather than refusing.

## 6. The in-plane tensor from azimuths, and σ33

$\sigma_{\varphi}$ is a quadratic form in $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$, so three azimuths not related
by $180^{\circ}$ determine $\sigma_{11}$, $\sigma_{22}$ and $\sigma_{12}$; the conventional set is
$0^{\circ}, 45^{\circ}, 90^{\circ}$, and further azimuths test the form. The in-plane principal
stresses are the eigenvalues of the $2\times2$ block,

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{I,II}} = \frac{\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22}}{2}
\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22}}{2}\right)^{2} + \sigma_{12}^{2}},
\qquad
\varphi_{\mathrm{I}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}(2\sigma_{12}, \sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22}),
$$

the extremes of the curve $\sigma_{\varphi}$ and the points where Mohr's circle meets the normal-stress
axis. $\varphi_{\mathrm{I}}$ is undefined when $\sigma_{\mathrm{I}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{II}}$, and its
propagated uncertainty then diverges, correctly.

**$\sigma_{33}$ and $d_{0}$ are inseparable.** In the fundamental equation $\sigma_{33}$ enters
through $\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}\sigma_{33}(1-\sin^{2}\psi) + S_{1}\sigma_{33}$ and $d_{0}$ through the
uniform strain it implies; with $\sigma_{11}$ and $\sigma_{22}$ free, a change of $\sigma_{33}$ is
exactly compensated by changes of $\sigma_{11}$, $\sigma_{22}$ and $d_{0}$. A triaxial evaluation
therefore needs an *independently measured* $d_{0}$, and the library refuses to refine $d_{0}$ in
one.

## 7. Diffraction elastic constants from single-crystal stiffness

Each grain-interaction model supplies the strain of a grain per unit macroscopic stress, a rank-4
map $\mathbf{P}_{g}$ in the crystal frame. The constants of the reflection follow by averaging over
the grains that diffract: those with the plane normal $\mathbf{n}$ along the scattering vector and
every rotation $\omega$ about it. That average is transversely isotropic about $\mathbf{n}$, so two
numbers describe it:

$$
S_{1} = \big\langle \mathbf{n}\mathbf{n} : \mathbf{P}_{g} : \mathbf{t}(\omega)\mathbf{t}(\omega)\big\rangle_{\omega},
\qquad
S_{1} + \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2} = \mathbf{n}\mathbf{n} : \mathbf{P}_{g} : \mathbf{n}\mathbf{n},
$$

with $\mathbf{t}(\omega)\perp\mathbf{n}$. The average is evaluated on 360 equally spaced $\omega$,
which integrates the degree-4 trigonometric integrand exactly. All tensor algebra is carried out
in **Mandel** (orthonormal) form, in which the inverse of the $6\times6$ matrix is the matrix of
the inverse tensor for stiffness and compliance alike, so no Voigt factors of 2 and 4 can be
misplaced. The plane normal is resolved through the reciprocal basis, $\mathbf{n}\propto\mathbf{B}^{*}\mathbf{h}$,
which is the only correct route outside the cubic system.

- **Reuss** (1929): every grain carries the macroscopic stress, $\mathbf{P}_{g} = \mathbf{S}_{g}$.
  For a cubic crystal this gives the closed forms, linear in the orientation parameter
  $\Gamma = (h^{2}k^{2}+k^{2}l^{2}+l^{2}h^{2})/(h^{2}+k^{2}+l^{2})^{2}$,
  $$
  S_{1} = S_{12} + S_{0}\Gamma,\qquad \tfrac{1}{2}S_{2} = S_{11} - S_{12} - 3S_{0}\Gamma,
  \qquad S_{0} = S_{11} - S_{12} - \tfrac{1}{2}S_{44},
  $$
  in Voigt compliance components; the implementation reproduces them to machine precision
  (`test_reuss_constants_match_the_cubic_closed_form`).
- **Voigt** (1928): every grain carries the macroscopic strain, so $\mathbf{P}_{g}$ is the
  compliance of the Voigt-averaged aggregate and the constants are reflection independent.
- **Neerfeld–Hill**: the arithmetic mean of the Reuss and Voigt constants (Neerfeld 1942;
  Hill 1952), an average of bounds that is not itself a bound.
- **Kröner** (1958): each grain is an Eshelby (1957) sphere embedded in the self-consistent
  effective medium. With Hill's constraint tensor of a sphere,
  $K^{*} = \tfrac{4}{3}G$ and $G^{*} = G(9K+8G)/(6(K+2G))$, the grain strain is
  $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{g} = \mathbf{A}_{g}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with
  $$
  \mathbf{A}_{g} = (\mathbf{C}_{g} + \mathbf{C}^{*})^{-1} : (\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{eff}} + \mathbf{C}^{*}),
  $$
  and $\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is iterated from the Hill average to the fixed point
  $\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{eff}} = \langle \mathbf{C}_{g}:\mathbf{A}_{g}\rangle$ (the orientation
  average being the isotropic projection $a\mathbf{J} + b\mathbf{K}$ with $a = \mathbf{J}::\mathbf{T}$,
  $b = \mathbf{K}::\mathbf{T}/5$). Then $\mathbf{P}_{g} = \mathbf{A}_{g}:\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}$.
  For cubic crystals the bulk modulus is exact, $K = (C_{11}+2C_{12})/3$, and the shear modulus is
  the positive root of Kröner's cubic
  $$
  G^{3} + \tfrac{5C_{11}+4C_{12}}{8}G^{2} - \tfrac{C_{44}(7C_{11}-4C_{12})}{8}G
  - \tfrac{C_{44}(C_{11}-C_{12})(C_{11}+2C_{12})}{8} = 0,
  $$
  which the iteration reproduces to $10^{-10}$ (`kroener_shear_modulus_cubic`).

The Reuss and Voigt constants bound the aggregate response and Kröner's lies between them; for
untextured cubic metals it is usually the closest to measured constants. The Reuss–Voigt spread
at the reflection used is a fair measure of how uncertain the constant is.

## 8. Locating the peak

Every stress in this note is computed from peak positions, and the uncertainty of each position is
its weight. Three location methods are offered (`locate_stress_peak`).

**Lorentz–polarization–absorption correction.** A stress peak is broad — several degrees on a
hardened steel — and across it the factor

$$
\mathrm{LPA}(2\theta) = \frac{1+\cos^{2}2\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}\times
\begin{cases}1 - \tan\psi\cot\theta & \omega\text{-tilting}\\ 1 & \chi\text{-tilting}\end{cases}
$$

changes enough to skew the profile, differently at each $\psi$ under $\omega$-tilting (SAE
HS-784). Dividing the counts by it, normalized at the window centre, removes the skew; the
counting uncertainties are divided with them.

**Pseudo-Voigt doublet fit (default).** The Kα1 line and its Kα2 partner at the Bragg-law
position $\sin\theta_{2} = (\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1})\sin\theta_{1}$, with shared width and mixing and
the tabulated intensity ratio, on a straight local background
(`pytex.diffraction.xrd_peaks.fit_peaks`). The position uncertainty is the fit covariance scaled
by the window's reduced $\chi^{2}$. Over 40 noise realizations of the demonstration measurement
the resulting stresses are unbiased and their normalized deviations have unit spread.

**Parabola through the peak top and continuous centroid (cross-checks).** Both see whatever
profile they are given, so the Kα2 line is first removed by Rachinger's recursion
$I_{1}(2\theta) = I(2\theta) - R\,I_{1}(2\theta')$ with the variance carried alongside,
$u^{2}(I_{1}) = u^{2}(I) + R^{2}u^{2}(I_{1}')$ (`pytex.diffraction.xrd_corrections.strip_kalpha2`).
This is not optional at stress angles: with Cr Kα at $2\theta\approx156^{\circ}$ the doublet is
$0.9^{\circ}$ apart and blended into a profile whose width grows as $1/\cos\psi$, so the bias of an
unstripped parabola changes from tilt to tilt and does not cancel in the slope. On the ferrite
(211) demonstration it moves $\sigma_{11}$ by more than 100 MPa
(`test_an_unstripped_doublet_biases_the_parabola`).

The parabola is a weighted least-squares fit through the points above 80 % of the net maximum;
its vertex $x_{0} = -b/2c$ has $u^{2}(x_{0}) = \mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}$ with
$\mathbf{g} = (0, -1/2c, b/2c^{2})$.

The centroid is taken over a window of fixed half-width (the interpolated half-maximum crossings)
**centred on the centroid itself**, with the profile integrated exactly as the piecewise-linear
interpolant of the points, so the result is continuous in the data. At a fixed window it is a ratio
of two linear functionals, $F = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{I}/\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{I}$; but the window
follows the answer, so the centroid is the fixed point $c = F(\mathbf{I}, c)$ and

$$
\frac{\partial c}{\partial I_{i}} = \frac{\partial F/\partial I_{i}}{1 - \partial F/\partial c}.
$$

With edges at half maximum $\partial F/\partial c\approx 0.6$; omitting the denominator understates
$u$ by a factor of about 2.5, which is exactly what a reduced $\chi^{2}$ near 6 then reports.

The top-of-peak methods estimate their background from the window ends, which Lorentzian tails
contaminate asymmetrically; over many realizations they carry a bias of one to two standard
uncertainties that the profile fit, which models the tails, does not. They are cross-checks.

A constant bias in position — a detector zero, a wavelength error — moves every tilt alike and
leaves the slopes, and so $\sigma_{\varphi}$, unchanged to first order. It does move the absolute
strain, and with it the $S_{1}$ term.

## 9. The least-squares fit and its uncertainty budget

**Propagation to strain.** $u(d) = d\cot\theta\,u(\theta)$ with $u(\theta) = u(2\theta)/2$ in radians,
and $u(\varepsilon) = u(d)/d_{0}$.

**Per-azimuth lines.** Weighted least squares of $d = c_{0} + c_{1}\sin^{2}\psi\,[+\,c_{2}\sin 2\psi]$
with weights $1/u^{2}(d)$ gives $\sigma_{\varphi}-\sigma_{33} = c_{1}/(d_{0}\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2})$ and
$\tau_{\varphi} = c_{2}/(d_{0}\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2})$. Linearity is tested by refitting with an added
$\sin^{4}\psi$ term: a coefficient beyond three of its standard uncertainties means $d$ is not
straight in $\sin^{2}\psi$ — a stress gradient within the penetration depth, or texture — and the
slope is then an average of something that is not a slope.

**The tensor.** With $\mathbf{W} = \operatorname{diag}(1/u^{2}(\varepsilon_{k}))$,

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\qquad
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{int}} = (\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A})^{-1},
\qquad
\chi^{2}_{\nu} = \frac{(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}})}{n-p}.
$$

The stress state fixes the free components: biaxial $(\sigma_{11},\sigma_{22},\sigma_{12})$; biaxial
with shear, adding $(\sigma_{13},\sigma_{23})$; triaxial, all six. Identifiability is checked from the
singular values of $\mathbf{A}$ before solving; a design that does not determine the tensor — a
single azimuth, most commonly — returns the per-azimuth stresses and says why no tensor is given.

**The budget** (JCGM 100:2008). The sources are independent and add in quadrature:

- *Statistical*: $\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{stat}} = \mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{int}}\max(1, \chi^{2}_{\nu})$. The
  Birge scaling widens the propagated uncertainty by the unexplained scatter and never narrows it.
  When the positions carried no uncertainty of their own (a pasted table without a fourth column)
  the scatter is all there is, and the factor is $\chi^{2}_{\nu}$ in both directions.
- *$d_{0}$*: the solution is linear in $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \mathbf{d}/d_{0} - 1$, so the
  sensitivity is exact, $\mathbf{s}_{d_{0}} = \mathbf{G}\,\partial\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}/\partial d_{0}$ with
  $\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{int}}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}$ and
  $\partial\varepsilon_{k}/\partial d_{0} = -d_{k}/d_{0}^{2}$; its contribution is
  $\mathbf{s}_{d_{0}}\mathbf{s}_{d_{0}}^{\mathsf T}u^{2}(d_{0})$.
- *Elastic constants*: the sensitivities to $S_{1}$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}$ (central differences of
  the linear solve) times their standard uncertainties, each $u(S)/S$ as declared.

The combined covariance $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{stat}} + \mathbf{V}_{d_{0}} + \mathbf{V}_{S}$
is propagated to every derived quantity — $\sigma_{\varphi}$ along any azimuth, the principal
stresses and their direction, the von Mises equivalent $\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}\mathbf{s}:\mathbf{s}}$ —
by its Jacobian. **Monte Carlo** (JCGM 101:2008): every input is redrawn from its uncertainty —
each $d_{k}$, $d_{0}$, $S_{1}$, $\tfrac{1}{2}S_{2}$ — and the linear system re-solved, in one batched
solve over all draws. Agreement of the Monte Carlo standard deviations with the combined
uncertainties confirms that the linearization holds; the test suite requires it to 6–15 %.

Correlations are reported from the combined covariance, but whether the measured directions
*separate* two components is judged on $\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{int}}$ alone: a dominant $u(d_{0})$ moves
$\sigma_{11}$ and $\sigma_{22}$ together by construction and would otherwise make every pair look
inseparable.

## 10. Determining d0 from the data

Under plane stress $d_{0}$ need not be given. The model $d = d_{0}[1 + \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}]$ is
bilinear, but in the variables $(d_{0}, d_{0}\mathbf{x})$ it is linear,

$$
d_{k} = d_{0} + \mathbf{a}_{k}\cdot(d_{0}\mathbf{x}),
$$

so one weighted linear solve gives both, and $\mathbf{x} = (d_{0}\mathbf{x})/d_{0}$ with its
covariance by the Jacobian of that division. The information on $d_{0}$ comes from requiring the
intercepts $d_{0}[1 + S_{1}(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22})]$ to agree with the slopes: it is the
strain-free-direction method of section 4, made exact and weighted. It fails, correctly, in a
triaxial evaluation (section 6).

## 11. Excluding measurements, and finding the ones to exclude

A single badly located peak — a fit that caught a spurious reflection, a specimen-height error at
one tilt, a detector artefact — pulls a least-squares fit towards itself. It does two kinds of
damage: it biases the tensor, and it inflates $\chi^{2}_{\nu}$ and with it, through the Birge
scaling, every uncertainty. The remedy is to leave the point out, and the method must make that
both easy and reviewable.

**Exclusion.** `determine_residual_stress(..., excluded=...)` takes indices of measurements to leave
out of *every* fit — the tensor, the per-azimuth lines, the budget and the Monte Carlo draws. The
excluded measurements stay in the result, flagged by `ResidualStressResult.included`, with the strain
the tensor fitted without them predicts; nothing is deleted, so the decision can be read in the
report and reversed. `indices_of_orientations` names measurements by $(\varphi, \psi)$, azimuth modulo
$360^{\circ}$, so an exclusion survives a change of peak-location method. In the workbench a point is
excluded by clicking it in the $d$ against $\sin^{2}\psi$ plot and pressing **Refit**; the plot draws
it as a red cross, and the report lists it with its residual.

**Which points?** The ordinary residual $\varepsilon_{i} - \mathbf{a}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is a poor guide: the fit
has already moved towards a bad point, shrinking its residual and enlarging those of its good
neighbours. On a ferrite measurement with one peak displaced by $0.25^{\circ}$, fourteen of 27 points
lie beyond $3u$ of the spoiled fit. The *deleted* (externally studentized) residual judges each point
against the fit made without it:

$$
t_{i} = \frac{\varepsilon_{i} - \mathbf{a}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{(i)}}
{\sqrt{s^{2}_{(i)}\left[u^{2}(\varepsilon_{i}) + \mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{V}_{(i)}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\mathsf T}\right]}},
\qquad s^{2}_{(i)} = \max\left(1, \chi^{2}_{\nu,(i)}\right),
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{(i)}$, $\mathbf{V}_{(i)}$ and $\chi^{2}_{\nu,(i)}$ come from the fit without
measurement $i$. The denominator is the standard uncertainty of the difference between an
independent measurement and a prediction, so under a correct model $t_{i}$ is close to a unit normal.
A bad point, compared with a fit that never saw it, stands out by a large factor. On the example
above it is the only point beyond 3.5, at $|t| \approx 90$, while no good point exceeds 2.7 over
three noise realizations. `ResidualStressResult.suggested_outliers` lists included points beyond 3.5
(about one good point in 2000 by chance); an excluded point keeps its $t_{i}$, now against the fit of
every included point, so a large value confirms the exclusion and a small one questions it.

**When not to exclude.** A point that is off because the *model* is wrong is information, not noise.
Systematic curvature of $d$ against $\sin^{2}\psi$ is a stress gradient; oscillation is texture;
branches that separate are shear. Removing such points makes the straight line fit and the answer
wrong. Exclusion is for measurements known or shown to be bad, with the reason recorded; the
suggestion is a prompt to inspect the peak fit, not a verdict.

## 12. Synchrotron measurements

At a synchrotron the wavelength is chosen, not given by an anode, and is usually short:
0.5 Å (24.8 keV) puts ferrite (211) at $2\theta \approx 25^{\circ}$ instead of $156^{\circ}$ with Cr K$\alpha$.
Three things change.

- **Strain sensitivity.** $\Delta(2\theta) = -2\tan\theta\,\varepsilon$, so the same stress moves the peak
  about eight times less at $25^{\circ}$ than at $156^{\circ}$. The far higher count rates, narrow
  instrumental profiles and freedom to choose high-index reflections compensate; the evaluation is
  unchanged.
- **Tilt geometry.** Under $\omega$-tilting the absorption factor $1 - \tan\psi\cot\theta$ is positive
  only while $\psi < \theta$: at larger tilts the incident or the diffracted beam runs below the
  surface. At $\theta = 12.4^{\circ}$ that leaves $\psi < 12.4^{\circ}$ — no usable $\sin^{2}\psi$ range — and
  `lpa_factor` refuses the geometry rather than returning a meaningless factor. Synchrotron stress
  work therefore uses $\chi$-tilting (or transmission), where the tilt axis lies in the diffraction
  plane and the absorption factor does not vary across the profile.
- **Polarization and no K$\alpha_2$.** The beam is one wavelength, so each peak is fitted as a single
  pseudo-Voigt, and the LPA factor uses the beam's polarization,
  $\mathrm{LP} = 2[f_{\perp} + (1 - f_{\perp})\cos^{2}2\theta]/\sin^{2}\theta$, with $f_{\perp} \approx 0.95$ for a
  vertical scattering plane (see {doc}`powder_xrd_and_saed`).

`test_a_synchrotron_measurement_recovers_the_stress_with_chi_tilting` generates a 0.5 Å,
$\chi$-tilted ferrite measurement and recovers the generating stress.

## 13. What is deliberately not modelled

- **Stress gradients.** The measured stress is the average over the X-ray penetration depth, which
  under $\omega$-tilting is
  $\tau = (\sin^{2}\theta - \sin^{2}\psi)/(2\mu\sin\theta\cos\psi)$ and falls with tilt. A steep
  gradient makes $d$ curved in $\sin^{2}\psi$; the curvature test flags it, and its evaluation
  needs the $\tau$- or scattering-vector method.
- **Texture.** In a textured material the diffracting grains at each $(\varphi,\psi)$ are a
  different population and $d$ oscillates in $\sin^{2}\psi$; the two-constant form no longer holds
  and stress factors $F_{ij}(\varphi,\psi,hkl)$ replace it.
- **Grain-interaction models beyond Kröner's sphere** (Vook–Witt for thin films, non-spherical
  Eshelby inclusions) and **intergranular (type II) stresses**, which shift peaks without a
  macroscopic stress.
- **Instrument alignment.** A specimen displacement shifts $2\theta$ by $-2s\cos\theta/R$, nearly the
  same at every tilt in $\chi$-geometry but not in $\omega$-geometry; practice is to verify
  alignment on a stress-free powder, whose apparent stress must be zero within its uncertainty
  (Fitzpatrick et al. 2005).
